10.3 Student Experiment Report

By Edward Carroll

Teacher: Mrs O’Connor

Word Count: 1180

Rationale:

Temperature is a major factor affecting reaction rates under collision theory–the theory that reactions require collisions to occur–alongside surface area, concentration, and catalysts (Augustyn, 2021). Reaction rate is the speed of a chemical reaction (Laidler, 2025). Increasing temperature increases the average kinetic energy of particles, increasing collision frequency, resulting in a higher reaction rate (How Does Temperature Relate to Collision Theory?, 2025) (Georgia Southern University, 2023). Activation energy is the minimum energy required for a chemical reaction to occur (How Does Temperature Relate to Collision Theory?, 2025). This increased kinetic energy may provide sufficient energy for particles to meet activation energy thresholds, leading to reactions.

The original class experiment investigated how hyd rochloric acid conc entrations affected calcium carbonate and reaction rates, identifying a relationship where higher hydrochloric acid concentrations resulted in increased reaction rates. However, the two concentrations and lack of repetitions provided insufficient data to identify a reliable trend.

The new experiment was redirected to investigate the relationships between temperature and reaction rates. Temperature was selected because the available equipment could quickly warm/cool materials to a broader range of temperatures. This improves trend reliability and extends the investigation to explore impacts of temperature on reaction rate.

Five temperatures (10°C, room temperature (26°C), 40°C, 60°C, 80°C) provided a broad range. Reaction time was recorded in seconds and converted to reaction rate.

Research Question: What is the correlation between temperature (10°C, 26°C, 40°C, 60°C, 80°C) and the reaction rate of a chemical reaction between 1M 10mL hydrochloric acid and 0.5g calcium carbonate?

Analysis of Data:

Table 1, Raw and processed results.

The graphs, trendline and R^2 value were made using Microsoft Excel (Figures 1-2). The trendline showed an overall positive linear correlation, although with moderate strength (R^2 = 0.4062) (Figure 1). Increasing temperatures generally increased individual and mean reaction rates (average 0.006 reaction rate at 10°C, 0.0375 at 80°C, 496% increase), as greater kinetic energy allows more particles to overcome activation energy and successfully collide, supported by collision theory (How Does Temperature Relate to Collision Theory?, 2025) (Table 1). The lowest individual reaction rate was 0.00364 at 10°C, compared to the highest rate, 0.09091, at 80°C, showing a 2400% increase. However, the 26°C mean reaction rate was 10.17% lower than at 10°C, deviating from the trend. This may be due to the 26°C tubes not having a controlled water bath, meaning the temperature was likely less controlled than others. Potential outliers include trial 2’s 80°C re action rate of 0.09091, compared with 0.028-0.029 values from trial 1 and 3, a percentage difference of 209-218%. This was potentially caused by uneven spreads of calcium carbonate, as surface area is one of the contributing factors under collision theory (Clark, 2013).

Table 2 presents trial and uncertainty values for each temperature. Percentage uncertainty was calculated by d ividing absolute uncertainty by the mean, allowing for group comparisons. Uncertainty varied by 51 percentage points between groups. The 26°C (27%), 40°C (16%) and 60°C (7%) groups had lower uncertainty. The 10°C (58%) and 80°C (45%) had greater uncertainty and should be treated cautiously, potentially the result of some tubes not meeting desired temperatures or losing temperature during transfer to experiment areas.

Evaluation:

Absolute percenta ge uncertainty, which represents the variability of data, suggests a moderate to high level of uncertainty, with values ranging from 7% to 58% and only a moderate fit with the trend (R^2 = 0.4062). This suggests that data should be treated with caution.

Several errors potentially contributed to high uncertainty at 10°C and 80°C. Some tubes did not reach the correct temperature, resulting in temperature deviations of up to 5°C. This potentially resulted in inconsistent reaction rates. Furthermor e, inaccuracies from using stopwatches as timers would have impacted the recorded times for 40°C values on trial 1 and 3.

Several measures ensured experiment validity. Three trials reduced the impacts of human error on results. One team member collected all reaction times for consistency. All variables other than temperature (eg. amount and concentration of hydrochloric acid) were controlled to ensure consistency throughout the experiment.

Several factors would have negatively impacted results. Temperatures may have changed between removal from the bath and the experiment area, particularly for the 10°C and 80°C tubes, changing the actual reaction temperature and results.

In addition, some tubes had up to 5°C of temperature differences, reducing reliability and weakening the trend and R^2 value.

Overall, the methodology was valid, but the data should be treated with caution due to these limitations.

To improve the validity of the data, thermometers could be used immediately before the experiment to ensure the hydrochloric acid is at the correct temperature. This would ensure reactions occur at the correct temperature, improving accuracy and addressing the temperature loss issue. Furthermore, tubes should only be used once they reach the required temperatures, directly addressing the inaccurate temperature issue and reducing temperature variability.

To extend how temperatures impact reaction rate, an experiment could investigate the influence of surface area compared to temperature on reaction rate.

Another extension could include identifying whether materials with different state of matter result in different reaction rates when exposed to different temperatures, identifying whether temperature impacts solids differently to liquids.

Conclusion:

The aim was to identify the correlation between temperatures and the reaction rate of a chemical reaction. A positive linear correlation was identified with moderate strength (R^2 = 0.4062), and a percentage increase of 496% in mean reaction rate from 10°C to 80°C. A limitation included tubes losing the desired temperature between the baths and the experiment area. This could be improved by measuring temperatures before the experiment to identify any temperature change. The data is valid overall but results should be treated with caution.
